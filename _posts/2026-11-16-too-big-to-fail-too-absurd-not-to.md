Who loves dim rooms and complimentary peanuts?  
If you shouted “drunks!” you’d be right. Turns out anarcho-capitalists do too.

“Trust me. It’ll pass, swipe it again.”

I met Marc at a Brooklyn cocktail bar in November 2022. He wore dizzyingly strong cologne, kept his hair, and waved around a transparent, silver-polished card stamped with BlockFi and Visa on the front.

“It’s been declined. Have you got another?” the bartender asked.

“It’s on the network—try again,” Marc insisted. After it was declined once more, he surrendered and paid with a red Bank of America card.

BlockFi was a crypto lender that collapsed after FTX, **[an exchange once valued at $32 billion](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/teaching-resources-library/sam-bankman-frieds-ftx)**, imploded in a famous fraud case. Their model: they loaned customers cash against their Bitcoin. Can’t pay? They keep your coins.

“What bank is that?” I asked.

“The future, bro. Bitcoin.” He smiled.  
  
The last thing I want to do at a bar is talk about digital currencies, but Marc proved to be the right person at the wrong time that night. I think most people have a basic understanding that our money is never truly safe. Without warning, banks can collapse,

but I figure most people simply refuse to make good financial decisions. From this day forward, I will test every “money” decision I make through what I am calling the **“Too big to fail, too absurd not to”** theory.

The idea that our investments can evaporate for either of two reasons: (1) we have blind faith in big companies, or (2) the world is absurd and enjoys messing with us in silly ways. A theory whose existence I will try to justify now.

## The “Too big to fail, too absurd not to” Theory

Normally, I wouldn’t give the time of day to some crypto bro in a dive bar, but his use of the word “network” intrigued me enough for both of us to have some words over the noise of the bar.

“What do you do?” I asked as the barman mixed our drinks. “Insurance sales now, but I used to run my own startup.” He responded proudly. He used to run a company selling powdered vegetable supplements after he graduated college. You know, superfood stuff. He had five employees until COVID hit his business particularly hard. Suddenly everyone wanted mashed potatoes, not broccoli dust.  
  
With sales declining, Marc was at risk of missing payroll payments

“Then I saw BlockFi ads everywhere. Even on my phone riding the train home.” He said.

Bitcoin still having roughly 700% of growing to do between its [November 2022 price](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/blockfi-bankruptcy-filing-november-28-190907854.html) and its [all-time high in October 2025](https://cryptobriefing.com/bitcoin-down-32-percent-year-after-record/) (still the record as I write this), Marc was able to use his coins to secure enough money to keep a float.

“I only bought myself another year before I had to close,” Marc said, rising from his seat to leave. “But I kept the account either way. The returns are insane.” Here is where the **first part** of the theory takes place:

### Marc thought his money was safe. For him, BlockFi was too big to fail.

People who lost their money in the 2008 housing crisis in the US thought the same. How could the system let these institutions with so much money crash so spectacularly?

Here’s what Marc didn’t know. What I didn’t know either. FTX blew up that same week, sending its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, to defend himself against fraud charges for the next couple of years, and eventually landing him a 25-year prison sentence.

FTX was the exchange that had **[extended BlockFi a $400 million credit line](https://pymnts.com/cryptocurrency/2022/blockfi-halts-withdrawals-in-ftx-fallout)** that summer, and BlockFi was exposed: **[$355 million in crypto on FTX’s platform](https://blockworks.co/news/blockfi-bankruptcy-highlights)**, plus a $671 million loan to Alameda Research, FTX’s sister trading firm. On November 10th, 2022, BlockFi was running out of money, and a reported **[650,000 accounts were suddenly frozen.](https://dailyhodl.com/2022/11/10/crypto-lending-platform-blockfi-announces-withdrawal-freeze-blames-lack-of-clarity-at-ftx-and-alameda-research/)**

And because the universe has a sense of humor: when the famous Silicon Valley Bank went under the following March, BlockFi turned out to have **[$227 million sitting in it, uninsured](https://www.theblock.co/post/218943/blockfi-has-227-million-in-uninsured-funds-in-silicon-valley-bank).** I repeat: in the space of five months, BlockFi got burned by its biggest partner and then had its own cash stuck in a failed bank.

I had met Marc in the precise week he became a casualty of an economy that loves to portray its institutions as immutable, only to watch them stumble dramatically, like someone slamming into a glass door they couldn’t see. Almost theatrically funny.

### Ueno Bank: A very silly, very dangerous game.

<figure>
<img src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/a8223477-113d-4908-bd86-cc348497edc3_1200x800.webp" alt="Storefront of a Ueno bank branch in Paraguay" >
</figure>

Let’s get recent now. Something happened in Paraguay earlier this month that’s worth knowing about, and it perfectly captures the **“Too absurd not to”** ethos of my theory.

Ueno was Paraguay’s shiny digital bank. It was founded in 2021, when a group of rich people bought an old-school finance company and rebuilt it as an app. By June it claimed 2.94 million customers, about 63% of the country’s adults, and a record-breaking $350 million from foreign investors. It offered cashback on groceries, won an award for “Best Customer Service Strategy” and handed out free MacBooks and iPads to its loyal customers. For a country whose GDP was about [$49 billion in 2025](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/08/28/pr26280-paraguay-imf-executive-board-concludes-2026-article-iv-consultation) (roughly 600 times smaller than the US), Ueno quickly became the bank with more customers than any other. Here is where the **second part** of the theory takes place:

### The world is absurd, and it enjoys messing with us in silly ways.

What Ueno was also doing, *allegedly*, was [lending money to companies tied to the bank’s own owners, beyond legal limits and without enough collateral](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/usa/how-one-banks-collapse-is-upending-paraguay-trapping-savings-and-threatening-its-president-nid05112026/). Paraguay’s president, Santiago Peña, [used to own shares in Ueno’s holding company](https://www.wunc.org/2026-11-09/how-a-bank-collapse-left-millions-in-limbo-and-toppled-paraguays-president), and he waved off the warnings as a smear campaign. My bet? Some of the companies that took Ueno’s money were passing it along to Peña, and the whole thing was one big circular loan to him. Nobody has proven that yet, but I’d put money on it (just not in Ueno). To make matters worse, the head of Paraguay’s Central Bank, the institution in charge of supervising the bank, used to *be* Ueno’s chairman. No joke. Once regulators found the hole, the app went dark and cards were declined everywhere. Within days, the Senate [removed Peña from office](https://www.riotimesonline.com/paraguay-ueno-bank-case-explained/) and prosecutors charged him with breach of trust. He denies any wrongdoing, and none of it has been tested in court.

From cashback on groceries to an ex-president facing criminal charges in less than a week.

And there it is. The **absurdity** of a company so integral to the future of a country, virtually inflating the economy on the back of YOUR money, or a shiny crypto card that promised to be the future that never existed, or money lent to friends of the house: BlockFi to FTX’s sister firm, Ueno to its owners’ own companies.

**That’s** the theory. The bigger something gets, the less willing people are to let it fail, and the more irrational its foundations get. Until they’re too absurd not to.

## Using the theory: What do people like Marc say about us?

Marc was right about Bitcoin. He was wrong about everything holding it.

Run them through the theory. Marc? Part one, blind faith in a big company that promised him the future. Ueno? Part two, a world so absurd that the bank’s old chairman ended up supervising the bank. Both too big to fail. Both too absurd not to.

Essentially, the powers that be are asleep at the wheel, and they are coming straight at us. Are we moving out of the way? No. We don’t. We’d rather sit on the ground and play marbles (at least I would, it’s been a while).

Another shiny card will come along, a little more translucent, with a better logo and a stadium deal, and we’ll hand it over and say: trust me, it will pass. Swipe it.

Humans are dumb, and we will continue to be dumb, even if we like to think we are elevated creatures.

Bummer.
